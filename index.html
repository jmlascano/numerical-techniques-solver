Systems of Linear Equations

Enter a system as its augmented matrix [A | b]. The solver uses Gaussian elimination to reach row echelon form, says whether the system is consistent, and finishes with back-substitution.

A
b

Each row is one equation: its coefficients go in A and its right-hand side in b. Entries may be integers (3), decimals (-0.25), or fractions (-3/4). With x, y, z selected, 4 unknowns are named w, x, y, z, and 5 or 6 unknowns use x₁ … xₙ.

LU Decomposition

Enter a square matrix A and a vector b. The solver writes A = LU using Anton’s method, then solves Ly = b and Ux = y.

A
b

Entries may be integers (3), decimals (-0.25), or fractions (-3/4). Leave b empty to find only L and U.

Determinants

Enter a square matrix A and choose a method: the definition (a sum over permutations), cofactor expansion along a row or column, or the adjoint.

A

Entries may be integers (3), decimals (-0.25), or fractions (-3/4). The definition method lists every permutation, so it is limited to 4 × 4.

Solution

Real Vector Spaces

Vectors in ℝⁿ, entered as columns. Check whether w is a linear combination of v₁, …, vₖ, whether the vectors span ℝⁿ, or whether they are linearly independent.

v₁ … vₖ
w

Each column is one vector: the first column is v₁, the second v₂, and so on. Entries may be integers (3), decimals (-0.25), or fractions (-3/4). Function spaces (the Wronskian) are not covered.

Solution

Coordinates and Bases

Test a basis, find coordinates, reduce or extend a set to a basis, or change basis. [v](B) means the coordinate vector of v relative to B, and P(B′→B) is the transition matrix from B′ to B, so [v](B) = P(B′→B)[v](B′).

Row, Column and Null Spaces

Enter any m × n matrix A. The solver reduces it to reduced row echelon form, then finds bases for the row space, column space and null space of A, its rank and nullity.

A

Entries may be integers (3), decimals (-0.25), or fractions (-3/4).

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Enter a square matrix A. The solver expands det(λIₙ − A) by cofactors, factors the characteristic polynomial exactly, and finds a basis for each eigenspace. It also gives the eigenvalues of Aᵏ and says whether A is invertible.

A

Entries may be integers (3), decimals (-0.25), or fractions (-3/4). k is a whole number from 1 to 50.

Solution

Diagonalization and Cayley–Hamilton

Diagonalize A as P⁻¹AP = D and compute Aᵏ = PDᵏP⁻¹, or verify the Cayley–Hamilton theorem p(A) = 0 and use it to find Aᵏ and A⁻¹.

A

Entries may be integers (3), decimals (-0.25), or fractions (-3/4). k is a whole number from 1 to 50.
